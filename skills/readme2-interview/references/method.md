# Method: how a professional interview works

Every practice below comes from a named source (full list in `sources.md`).
Anything not sourced here is not part of the method.

## 1. Framing
Decide the angle, the audience and the output before anything else. The output decides
what to collect: an article needs a narrative thread and quotes; later formats (posts,
podcast, video) need short quotes and visual moments.

## 2. Research before the interview
- Research the person, the topic and the context from primary and secondary sources
  before the interview. (Oral History Association)
- Inventory the documents first, and build personalised questions from them; generic
  questions get stock answers. (parents-memoir)
- Keep a timeline that shows the periods with no recorded story, and a list of
  contradictions to clarify. (Heirloom)

## 3. Pre-interview
- Learn the narrator's expectations, the topics that matter to them and the questions
  they would like to be asked. Agree on session length, declined topics and what will
  be public. (Oral History Association)
- In television and radio, a producer runs a short pre-interview call to test the
  content and the energy, and passes useful remarks to the host. (Throughline Group)

## 4. Interview guide
- An open-ended guide of themes and general questions, not a fixed questionnaire.
  (Oral History Association)
- Choose one organising structure before you begin: timeline, topic, life event, or a
  hybrid. (Storii)
- Order and linking of questions: see `questions.md`.

## 5. The interview
- Open with easy questions, ask open-ended questions, listen actively. (Oral History
  Association; jamditis interview-prep)
- Ask about a specific past instance ("tell me about the last time…") rather than about
  habits; specific stories are more reliable than generalisations. (Teresa Torres)
- Ask follow-up questions for clarification, elaboration and reflection; probe
  respectfully instead of accepting superficial answers. (Oral History Association)
- Challenge statements with evidence when they conflict with what you know. (jamditis
  interview-prep)
- Let the narrator go beyond the project's focus when that is where the relevant
  material is. (Oral History Association)
- Close by asking what you missed. (jamditis interview-prep)
- Several sessions are normal: the Paris Review interviews ran over two or three
  sittings. (Malcolm Cowley, via The Marginalian)

## 6. Debrief
- Block time right after the interview to debrief while memory is fresh. (Umbrex)
- Three blocks: what we learned, how much we trust it, what changes. (Umbrex)
- Label each statement fact, inference or opinion. Quotation marks only for
  near-verbatim language; everything else is paraphrase. (Umbrex)
- Note what was surprising, contradictory or uncertain, and what new questions emerged.
  (Umbrex)

## 7. Processing and validation
- Back up the record and transcribe key quotes the same day; fact-check within 48 hours;
  confirm attribution and quotes before publication. (jamditis interview-prep)
- Cut to length and arrange in logical order, then send to the subject for approval.
  (Paris Review, via The Marginalian)
- Give narrators an opportunity to approve before public release. (Oral History
  Association)
- Uncertain facts stay blank, marked `[?]`, and any stated reason must trace back to
  something the person actually said. (parents-memoir)

## 8. Outputs
- A well-run 45-minute interview should yield 5 to 10 quotable moments, 2 to 4 methods,
  3 to 5 personal stories, and 1 or 2 contrarian positions. (ud-a)
- Interviews that go deep on specific tactics and outcomes produce more usable material
  than general advice. (ud-a)
- Article rules: see `formats.md`.
